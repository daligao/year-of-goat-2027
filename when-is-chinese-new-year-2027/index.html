Why the Date Moves Every Year

Chinese New Year doesn't fall on a fixed date because it follows a lunisolar calendar rather than the purely solar Gregorian calendar used for January 1. A lunar month runs roughly 29.5 days, and 12 of them add up to about 354 days — 11 days short of a solar year.

To keep the calendar in sync with the seasons, an extra "leap month" is added roughly every three years. The result: Chinese New Year always falls on the second new moon after the winter solstice, which keeps it anchored somewhere between January 21 and February 20 on the Western calendar — but never on the same date twice in a row.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the date change every year?

Chinese New Year follows a lunisolar calendar, not the fixed 365-day solar calendar most of the world uses. It falls on the second new moon after the winter solstice, so the date drifts across the Western calendar, always landing somewhere between January 21 and February 20.

What animal is 2027?

2027 is the Year of the Goat — specifically the Fire Goat, since the Chinese calendar also cycles through five elements (Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water) alongside the 12 animals. In Chinese it's written 丁未年. The last Fire Goat year was 1967, 60 years earlier.

Is Chinese New Year a public holiday in China?

Yes. Chinese New Year (Spring Festival) is China's most important public holiday, typically observed as a multi-day "golden week" with most businesses, schools, and government offices closed. Exact official dates for 2027 are usually announced by China's State Council in the preceding autumn or winter.

How long do celebrations last?

Traditionally, Chinese New Year celebrations run for 15 days, from New Year's Eve through the Lantern Festival. Most public holidays and family gatherings concentrate in the first 3-7 days, with the Lantern Festival marking the formal close of the celebration.
